🎯 Objective

Neural NetworksPattern RecognitionChatGPTPrompt Engineering
📖 The Lesson

When people say AI is "thinking," they're wrong — and understanding why makes you a better engineer. AI doesn't think. It predicts. A language model like ChatGPT has processed billions of sentences. When you type words, it calculates: given everything before this, what word is statistically most likely to come next? That calculation happens thousands of times per second to produce a response. You are not talking to a mind. You are talking to a very fast probability engine.

The mechanism is a Neural Network — layers of mathematical operations loosely inspired by how neurons connect and signal. After training, it recognizes patterns in your input and produces the word pattern most likely to be a useful response. This is why ChatGPT can be confidently, fluently wrong. It is not lying — it is predicting. There is no internal fact-checker. There is only probability.

Gaming connection: Enemy AI in video games doesn't "think" either — it follows decision trees and probability weights. A boss that "reads" your moves is executing a script that checks recent inputs and selects a counter-move by weighted probability. Same principle as ChatGPT: sophisticated pattern matching, not reasoning. Understanding this is what separates people who use AI from people who build AI systems.

📖 The Lesson

Fusion 360 is the industry-standard CAD tool for mechanical engineers and product designers. Autodesk's free Personal Use license includes full solid modeling, assemblies, and the Simulation workspace (FEA) — everything you need for this program. Today you use the Solid workspace to build a phone stand with three components: a base, a back support, and a phone cradle.

Good engineering design means making intentional, defensible decisions — not just "it looks right" but "I made the base 4 inches wide because that keeps the center of gravity inside the footprint when a 200-gram phone is in the cradle." Every dimension should have a reason. Start building that habit today.

Setup first: Go to autodesk.com/fusion360 → click "For Personal Use" → create a free Autodesk account. Installation takes 10–15 minutes. Have it installed before your 60-minute session starts. Search YouTube for "Fusion 360 beginner sketch extrude 5 minutes" if you've never opened it before.
⚡ Activity — 60 Minutes
Platform: Autodesk Fusion 360 (free Personal Use tier · autodesk.com/fusion360)
Design a 3-Component Gaming Phone Stand
1
(10 min) — Orientation. Open Fusion 360 in the Solid workspace. Three tools you'll use today: Sketch (draw 2D shapes on a plane), Extrude (push a 2D sketch into a 3D solid), and Fillet (round off sharp corners). Watch a quick YouTube tutorial if needed — "Fusion 360 sketch extrude beginner" will show you the basics in under 5 minutes.
2
(15 min) — Component 1: The Base. Create a new component called "Base." Sketch a rectangle on the top plane — 4 inches wide × 3 inches deep. Extrude it 0.4 inches up. Engineering decision: why 4 inches wide? A phone weighs ~200 grams. If the cradle is 2 inches back from the front edge, a 4-inch base keeps the center of gravity safely inside the footprint so it won't tip over.
3
(15 min) — Component 2: The Back Support. Create a new component called "Back Support." Sketch a rectangle on the back edge of the base — 0.4 inches wide × 3.5 inches tall. Extrude it 0.4 inches forward. This vertical wall bears the main rearward force from the phone leaning against it. Make it at least 0.4 inches thick — thinner and it risks cracking under repeated load.
4
(10 min) — Component 3: The Phone Cradle. Create a component called "Cradle." Sketch a small ledge off the back support — 1 inch deep × 3 inches wide. Extrude it 0.3 inches. This is what the phone's bottom edge rests on. A slight upward angle (5–10°) pushes the phone back against the support rather than away from it.
5
(5 min) — Apply Fillets. Use the Fillet tool to round the top corners of the back support and the front edge of the base. Even 0.1-inch fillets matter: sharp corners are stress concentration points — where cracks start under repeated loading. You'll see exactly why in Week 3's FEA lesson.
6
(5 min) — Screenshot. Take two screenshots: isometric view (corner angle showing all 3 components) and front view. Label both in your Google Doc.

Hallucination, Bias & Who Gets Hurt

🎯 Objective

Understand AI hallucination and demonstrate it yourself. Study the Amazon hiring AI bias case. Explain why AI bias is an engineering responsibility problem — not just a PR problem.

Perplexity AIHallucinationAI BiasEngineering Ethics
📖 The Lesson

AI hallucination happens because LLMs don't know what is true — they know what is statistically plausible. A model trained on millions of sentences beginning "Abraham Lincoln said..." will generate convincing Lincoln quotes he never said. The model is not lying. It has no concept of truth. It is predicting the most likely word sequence given your prompt.

AI bias is a deeper problem. In 2018, Amazon shut down an AI hiring tool after discovering it systematically downgraded résumés containing the word "women's" — as in "women's chess team." The model had been trained on 10 years of Amazon's own hiring history from a male-dominated industry. It learned that male-pattern résumés correlated with success. The engineers had no discriminatory intent. But they shipped a discriminatory product because they didn't audit the training data.

These are not hypotheticals. Facial recognition tools deployed by U.S. law enforcement have misidentified Black men at significantly higher error rates, contributing to wrongful arrests. Automated lending systems have charged higher rates in majority-Black neighborhoods with equal creditworthiness. These happened because engineers didn't ask "who could this harm?" before deployment. As a future engineer building AI products, that question is part of your professional responsibility.

🎯 Objective

Chain-of-ThoughtChatGPTMaster PromptEngineering Reasoning
📖 The Lesson

By default, AI jumps to the most statistically likely answer — fast but often wrong for multi-step problems. Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting fixes this by explicitly instructing the model to work through the problem step by step before reaching a conclusion. When each intermediate step is generated, it conditions the next token prediction — dramatically improving accuracy on engineering calculations, logical analysis, and business decisions.
